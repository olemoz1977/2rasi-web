# WorkStyle 8-axis — Stage 0 Pole Definitions

Status: STAGE 0 / CONSTRUCT & POLE DEFINITIONS / NOT VALIDATED
Date: 2026-08-23

## Purpose

Define the behavioural space and both legitimate poles for each of the 8 WorkStyle candidate axes before candidate-item writing.

Each axis must answer one recurring work-style question. Neither pole is a competence label or a preferred employer answer.

---

# Axis 1 — Structured Preparation ↔ Emergent Organisation

## Shared behavioural space
How work is organised when there is meaningful freedom over the amount of advance structure.

## Pole A — Structured Preparation
Typical orientation:
- clarify sequence before starting;
- define steps / checkpoints early;
- reduce ambiguity through advance organisation;
- prefer explicit structure before execution expands.

Functional value:
- coordination;
- repeatability;
- fewer omissions;
- easier handoffs.

Possible cost at an extreme:
- planning overhead;
- slower start;
- over-structuring uncertain work.

## Pole B — Emergent Organisation
Typical orientation:
- begin with enough structure to move;
- organise details while learning from execution;
- allow sequence and routines to emerge;
- avoid unnecessary advance formalisation.

Functional value:
- speed;
- responsiveness;
- lower setup overhead;
- fit to uncertain work.

Possible cost at an extreme:
- inconsistency;
- coordination gaps;
- omitted dependencies.

## Same-space test
PASS.
Both poles answer: `When do I create work structure?`

## Simultaneous-high risk
Low when referent is held constant.

## Desirability risk
Moderate. Organisational culture may praise planning. Item contexts must show situations where overplanning is costly.

## Nearest neighbour
Method Consistency ↔ Method Adaptation.

## Exclusion rule
Axis 1 is about **creating structure**, not changing an already functioning method because of feedback.

## Current status
**STRONG / ADVANCE TO ITEM CANDIDATE DEVELOPMENT.**

# Axis 2 — Method Consistency ↔ Method Adaptation

## Shared behavioural space
How readily a person preserves versus modifies a currently used work method while the underlying objective remains broadly stable.

## Pole A — Method Consistency
Typical orientation:
- preserve a known method long enough to stabilise it;
- avoid changing routines without sufficient reason;
- value repeatability and accumulated learning;
- prefer controlled rather than continuous adjustment.

Functional value:
- reliability;
- comparable outcomes;
- standardisation;
- learning from repeated execution.

Possible cost at an extreme:
- slow response to changing conditions;
- unnecessary rigidity.

## Pole B — Method Adaptation
Typical orientation:
- modify the way work is done as useful feedback appears;
- tune approach to changing conditions;
- experiment with adjustments during execution;
- prefer fit to context over preserving one method.

Functional value:
- responsiveness;
- contextual fit;
- experimentation;
- recovery from changing conditions.

Possible cost at an extreme:
- excessive switching;
- unstable standards;
- difficulty learning whether a method actually works.

## Same-space test
PASS.
Both poles answer: `How stable should my current method remain?`

## Simultaneous-high risk
Low to moderate. A person can value standards and adaptation in different contexts; scenarios must use comparable uncertainty/risk.

## Desirability risk
Moderate. `Adaptable` sounds positive. Avoid stems where current method is explicitly failing.

## Nearest neighbour
Structured Preparation; Conclusion Revision.

## Exclusion rule
The object being changed must be **the method / way of working**, never the person's conclusion or the work objective.

## Current status
**STRONG / ADVANCE.**

# Axis 3 — Local / Concrete Focus ↔ System / Relational Focus

## Shared behavioural space
The default scope at which a person initially frames a work issue when both immediate and broader analysis are plausible.

## Pole A — Local / Concrete Focus
Typical orientation:
- isolate the immediate issue;
- focus on the concrete part currently affected;
- prefer actionable local detail before widening the frame;
- contain the problem before exploring broader connections.

Functional value:
- speed;
- precision;
- containment;
- reduced analytical overhead.

Possible cost at an extreme:
- missed dependencies;
- recurring symptoms treated locally.

## Pole B — System / Relational Focus
Typical orientation:
- scan dependencies and recurring patterns;
- ask what else may be affected;
- widen the frame before deciding where to intervene;
- notice interactions across parts of work.

Functional value:
- downstream-awareness;
- root-pattern detection;
- prevention of local optimisation.

Possible cost at an extreme:
- slower action;
- analysis beyond what the issue warrants;
- diffusion of attention.

## Same-space test
PASS conceptually.
Both poles answer: `How wide is my first frame?`

## Simultaneous-high risk
Moderate. Skilled people can zoom in and out. WorkStyle must target **default first orientation**, not capability.

## Desirability risk
HIGH if called `systems thinking` because system focus sounds more sophisticated.

## Nearest neighbour
Opportunity ↔ Risk Scanning.

## Exclusion rule
Hold valence constant. Axis 3 is about **scope**, not whether attention goes toward positive possibilities or negative risks.

## Current status
**PROMISING / ADVANCE ONLY WITH HIGH-CHALLENGE ITEM DESIGN.**

Kill criterion:
If cognitive participants interpret Local Focus as `not seeing the big picture` or System Focus as `being smarter`, drop or redesign the axis.

# Axis 4 — Conclusion Stability ↔ Evidence-led Revision

## Shared behavioural space
How readily an already formed work conclusion is reopened when credible but non-decisive new evidence appears.

## Pole A — Conclusion Stability
Typical orientation:
- require substantial evidence before reopening a settled conclusion;
- protect decisions from constant reconsideration;
- prefer continuity once enough evidence has supported a view;
- avoid changing interpretation after every new signal.

Functional value:
- decisiveness;
- consistency;
- resistance to noise;
- execution stability.

Possible cost at an extreme:
- delayed correction;
- confirmation bias / overcommitment.

## Pole B — Evidence-led Revision
Typical orientation:
- reopen conclusions relatively readily when credible counter-evidence appears;
- keep interpretations provisional longer;
- update judgement as new information accumulates;
- prefer responsiveness to evidence over continuity of view.

Functional value:
- error correction;
- learning from new information;
- reduced lock-in.

Possible cost at an extreme:
- repeated reconsideration;
- decision instability;
- overreaction to noisy evidence.

## Same-space test
PASS conceptually.
Both poles answer: `How much evidence is enough to reopen a conclusion?`

## Simultaneous-high risk
Moderate. Good decision makers can be both appropriately stable and appropriately revisable depending on evidence quality.

## Desirability risk
HIGH. `Open-minded / evidence-led` is socially attractive.

## Nearest neighbours
Method Adaptation; Direct Disagreement.

## Exclusion rules
- no method/action change as the primary object;
- no interpersonal disagreement without new evidence;
- no obviously decisive evidence where revision is objectively correct.

## Current status
**FRAGILE CONDITIONAL / ADVANCE TO SMALL POLE TEST ONLY.**

Kill criterion:
Drop from V1 if respondents consistently describe the axis as `smart people change when facts change` or cannot imagine legitimate value in a higher revision threshold.

# Axis 5 — Opportunity Scanning ↔ Risk Scanning

## Shared behavioural space
What receives attention first when evaluating an uncertain option with both potential upside and potential downside.

## Pole A — Opportunity Scanning
Typical orientation:
- notice possible value, openings and upside first;
- explore what could become possible;
- generate potential routes before focusing on failure conditions.

Functional value:
- idea generation;
- initiative;
- opportunity recognition;
- forward movement.

Possible cost at an extreme:
- underweighting constraints;
- premature enthusiasm.

## Pole B — Risk Scanning
Typical orientation:
- notice failure conditions, constraints and downside first;
- test what could break;
- identify exposure before committing attention to upside.

Functional value:
- prevention;
- realism;
- quality / safety awareness;
- robustness.

Possible cost at an extreme:
- missed opportunities;
- delayed exploration;
- defensive narrowing.

## Same-space test
PASS conceptually if the same uncertain option is evaluated.

## Simultaneous-high risk
Moderate to high. Thorough people can scan both rapidly. Instrument must measure **first attentional orientation**, not total ability.

## Desirability risk
Moderate. `Opportunity` may sound innovative; `risk` may sound responsible. This relative symmetry is useful.

## Nearest neighbour
Local/System Focus.

## Exclusion rule
Hold **scope** constant. The axis contrasts attentional direction, not breadth of analysis.

## Current status
**CONDITIONAL / ADVANCE TO POLE TEST.**

Kill criterion:
If respondents answer almost entirely according to industry/role risk rather than recognise a usual personal first orientation, park the axis as role-context rather than WorkStyle.

# Axis 6 — Autonomous Execution ↔ Collaborative Coordination

## Shared behavioural space
How work/decisions are coordinated when both independent and collaborative routes are viable.

## Pole A — Autonomous Execution
Typical orientation:
- proceed independently when coordination is not necessary;
- prefer personal ownership of the work path;
- minimise coordination overhead;
- consult when a clear need exists rather than by default.

Functional value:
- speed;
- ownership;
- reduced meeting / coordination cost;
- deep individual focus.

Possible cost at an extreme:
- information silos;
- missed alignment;
- late involvement of others.

## Pole B — Collaborative Coordination
Typical orientation:
- involve relevant people earlier;
- prefer alignment while work is developing;
- share information and decisions more actively;
- use collective input even when independent execution is possible.

Functional value:
- shared context;
- integration;
- broader input;
- smoother handoffs.

Possible cost at an extreme:
- coordination overhead;
- slower decision flow;
- diluted ownership.

## Same-space test
PASS.

## Simultaneous-high risk
Low if items explicitly say both routes are viable.

## Desirability risk
Moderate because `teamwork` is socially valued. Autonomous pole must be presented as efficient ownership, not isolation.

## Nearest neighbours
Direct Disagreement; Expressive Participation.

## Exclusion rule
No conflict and no requirement for public/social activity. The only question is **who is involved in doing/deciding**.

## Current status
**STRONG / ADVANCE.**

# Axis 7 — Direct Disagreement ↔ Alignment-seeking

## Shared behavioural space
How a person handles an interpersonal difference when there is no decisive factual answer forcing one side to be correct.

## Pole A — Direct Disagreement
Typical orientation:
- name the difference explicitly;
- make own concern / preference visible early;
- tolerate interpersonal friction to clarify the issue;
- prefer explicit disagreement over indirect accommodation.

Functional value:
- clarity;
- boundary visibility;
- faster surfacing of conflict;
- less hidden disagreement.

Possible cost at an extreme:
- unnecessary friction;
- reduced relational sensitivity.

## Pole B — Alignment-seeking
Typical orientation:
- first search for shared ground and workable alignment;
- soften the expression of differences when stakes allow;
- preserve relationship / coordination while negotiating disagreement;
- yield selectively when direct confrontation adds little value.

Functional value:
- relationship preservation;
- consensus;
- reduced friction;
- smoother cooperation.

Possible cost at an extreme:
- important disagreement may remain hidden;
- delayed boundary-setting.

## Same-space test
PASS.

## Simultaneous-high risk
Low to moderate. Skilled communicators can do both sequentially; instrument targets which move tends to come first.

## Desirability risk
Moderate. Directness can sound courageous, alignment can sound collaborative. Both poles have positive language available.

## Nearest neighbours
Conclusion Revision; Autonomous/Collaborative.

## Exclusion rules
- disagreement must be interpersonal;
- do not introduce decisive new evidence;
- do not make collaboration itself the outcome.

## Current status
**STRONG / ADVANCE.**

# Axis 8 — Expressive Participation ↔ Reserved Participation

## Shared behavioural space
The amount of visible conversational/social space a person tends to occupy in ordinary group interaction.

## Pole A — Expressive Participation
Typical orientation:
- enter discussion readily;
- make reactions / enthusiasm visible;
- speak early and often enough to shape group energy;
- think / engage partly through interaction.

Functional value:
- visible engagement;
- momentum;
- rapid social information exchange;
- energising interaction.

Possible cost at an extreme:
- crowding others;
- reduced reflection before speaking.

## Pole B — Reserved Participation
Typical orientation:
- observe longer before entering;
- speak selectively;
- keep more reaction internal;
- prefer contribution when there is a clear reason to add it.

Functional value:
- listening;
- selectivity;
- lower social noise;
- more reflection before contribution.

Possible cost at an extreme:
- useful input remains invisible;
- others may underestimate engagement.

## Same-space test
PASS.

## Simultaneous-high risk
Low when ordinary group interaction is specified.

## Desirability risk
Low to moderate if reserved pole is written as selectivity rather than withdrawal.

## Nearest neighbours
Autonomous/Collaborative; Direct Disagreement.

## Exclusion rule
No disagreement and no need for collaboration. Measure **visibility/intensity of participation only**.

## Current status
**STRONG / ADVANCE.**
