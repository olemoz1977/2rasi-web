Situational Leadership

Leadership is not one fixed style. This experiment asks how your response shifts when the person, task and level of uncertainty change.

What it explores

The same leadership behaviour can be useful in one situation and counterproductive in another. The experiment presents practical situations and looks at which type of response you tend to choose.

The focus is flexibility: whether you can increase direction, support, participation or autonomy when the situation calls for it.

Four response directions

S1 · DirectingMore structure, clearer instructions and closer guidance when clarity or competence is low.
S2 · CoachingDirection plus explanation, encouragement and dialogue while capability is developing.
S3 · SupportingLess task direction and more listening, involvement and confidence-building.
S4 · DelegatingMore autonomy and ownership when competence and confidence are already strong.

Why the boundary situations?

Three additional scenarios — crisis, expert autonomy and safety — are there to test whether your usual tendency changes when the context becomes more extreme.

They are not a diagnostic “flexibility score”. They are prompts to notice whether you can move away from your default response when the situation clearly changes.

How to read the result

Do not search for the “best style”. Look at the distribution of your choices. A mixed profile can be more informative than a single dominant label.

This is an independent situational leadership reflection tool, not a validated diagnostic instrument or an official assessment from a commercial leadership programme.

Which response do you reach for first?

Choose what you would actually do in each situation, especially when the most polished answer feels obvious.
